EGT predicts how a robot will actually move over rough terrain,
then uses those predictions to choose paths it is likely to complete.

A route can look safe on a terrain map and still cause a robot to slip, get stuck, or tip over. EGT predicts several possible motions for each candidate path and estimates how likely the robot is to follow it successfully. It accounts for both the terrain and the robot’s feedback controller.

02

The problem: a planned path is not the actual motion

Many traversability checks look at the robot’s tilt or stability along a planned route. But they can miss what happens when the robot and its controller actually try to follow it.

Recovery

A brief tilt can be recoverable.

A robot can briefly tilt and still recover. Rejecting a path just because of that moment can rule out a route the robot could complete.

Progress

Staying upright is not enough.

A robot may stay upright but slip, stop moving, or slide toward a ditch. Acceptable tilt angles do not guarantee that it reaches the end.

Deviation

The robot may leave the reference.

Uneven ground and steering corrections can push the robot away from the planned line. The resulting motion is what needs to be assessed.

EGT checks two things together

The robot must stay close enough to the path and make enough progress toward its endpoint. A prediction counts as successful only when both happen.

The EGT probability

pEGT = P(z⊥ ≤ 1 ∧ ρ ≥ β | C)

z⊥ ≤ 1: stay inside the tracking corridor. ρ ≥ β: complete enough of the path. C: the route, terrain, and robot context.

EGT is the fraction of the n predicted executions that meet both requirements.

03

How EGT works

For each possible route, EGT predicts how the robot will move while its controller follows that route. It generates several complete motions, scores their success, and uses that score to help select the next path.

  1. Look at the route

    Read the terrain around the candidate path, the robot’s expected posture and wheel contacts, and the path endpoint.

  2. Predict possible motions

    A learned flow-matching model generates n possible executions of the robot following this path.

  3. Count successful predictions

    Check which motions stay close to the route and reach far enough along it. Keep a candidate when its estimated success probability meets the chosen threshold.

  4. Choose a path and repeat

    Among the accepted paths, favor progress toward the goal while accounting for the risk of poor tracking or stalled progress. Execute the selected path, then replan.

Architecture and training overview

Understand the terrain and route. A terrain encoder extracts features around the candidate path. The robot’s expected posture, wheel contacts, and path endpoint provide additional context.

Learn possible executions. A trajectory autoencoder compresses complete paths into a compact representation. A conditional flow-matching model learns to generate execution representations from noise, and the decoder turns them back into full trajectories.

Train from recorded motion. Reference paths are paired with the motions actually recorded while the simulated robot follows them. This teaches the model how the vehicle and its controller respond together to the terrain.

04

Results

Traversability classification on 650 simulated executions over terrains absent from training: 598 successful and 52 unsuccessful executions.

Knowing the robot’s posture and wheel contacts matters.

Removing this information increases the share of failed executions incorrectly accepted from 7.7% to 71.2%. The ablation uses a separately trained flow-matching model with the same training data and procedure.

How were the baselines evaluated?

GP-navigation uses its author-provided online implementation. TAL is trained from the same underlying simulated executions as EGT and uses an adapted π-MPPI controller. The comparison evaluates complete navigation systems with different prediction targets and planning objectives.

Do predicted probabilities match actual success rates?

EGT is conservative in the lowest probability bin and slightly overestimates success when it predicts probability one. Group-level calibration is measured using the same 650 executions, with one actual execution per reference.

06

Takeaway

Traversability depends on how a robot and its controller respond to the terrain. A brief tilt may be recoverable, while an upright robot may still get stuck. Predicting the distribution of closed-loop trajectories helps distinguish paths the robot can traverse from those it cannot.
